Lesson 09 · The key of D

One scale, six chords, three ways to play each

The major scale as a recipe, the chords that fall out of it, and inversions: the same chord with a different note on the bottom.

The major scale is a recipe

A major scale isn't eight notes to memorise. It's a pattern of steps, and the pattern works from any key. A whole step skips one key: two semitones, counting black keys. A half step goes to the very next key: one semitone.

The recipe W · W · H · W · W · W · H

Whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half. Seven steps take you from the starting note up to the same letter an octave higher. The two half steps always fall between notes 3–4 and 7–8.

Start on D and follow it:

So D major is D E F♯ G A B C♯ D. It has two sharps, F♯ and C♯, and you didn't have to memorise either one. The recipe forced them.

Six chords from one scale

Every note of the scale can be the bottom of a chord. Take a scale note, skip one, take the next, skip one, take the next. That's the same stacking-thirds rule from Lesson 6, but using only the notes of D major. The chord's quality then comes out by itself:

Major or minor — the chord rule Major: root, up 4 semitones to the third, up 3 more to the fifth.
Minor: root, up 3, then up 4.

Same two gaps, opposite order. Count the keys, black ones included.

Look at the quality column: major, minor, minor, major, major, minor. That pattern is the same in every major key. In C it's C, Dm, Em, F, G, Am, and Lesson 6's four chords were numbers 1, 4, 5 and 6 of that list. In D, the same four are D, G, A, Bm.

Inversions: same chord, different note on the bottom

A chord is its three letters, not the order you stack them in. D, F♯ and A make a D chord whichever one is lowest. Changing which note is on the bottom is called an inversion, and every three-note chord has three positions:

PositionOn the bottomOrder, low → highD chord
Root positionthe root (1)1 · 3 · 5D · F♯ · A
1st inversionthe third (3)3 · 5 · 1F♯ · A · D
2nd inversionthe fifth (5)5 · 1 · 3A · D · F♯
How to find one Play the chord in root position. Then take the bottom note and move it up an octave, to the top. That's 1st inversion. Do it again and you have 2nd inversion. Do it a third time and you're back in root position, one octave higher.

The gap is the practical trick. In root position the three notes are evenly spaced. In each inversion, one gap is wider (a fourth instead of a third). In 1st inversion it's at the top; in 2nd inversion it's at the bottom. Your hand can feel that without you naming a single note.

Why bother: chords that hardly move

This is what inversions are for. Play D, G, A and Bm all in root position and your right hand jumps up and down the keyboard. Choose the right inversion of each, and the hand barely moves:

ChordRight handPositionLeft hand (held)
DD · F♯ · ArootD octave
GD · G · B2nd inversionG octave
AC♯ · E · A1st inversionA octave
BmD · F♯ · B1st inversionB octave

Your thumb stays on D for three of the four chords. It's exactly the pattern from Lesson 8, moved to D: the left hand holds an octave, and the right hand plays the chord four times above it. The left hand plays each chord's root, so the right hand is free to use whichever inversion sits closest.

Check yourself

Between now and next time

Ten minutes. Two on the scale, saying the steps out loud. Three on symbol → chord until all six come without counting. Three on the inversion walk. Two on D – G – A – Bm, no clock. Bring the click in once the left hand is holding four bars out of four.

Then try the walk on chords outside this lesson, like C, F and Am from Lesson 6. The rule is the same for every chord: bottom note to the top.
