Lesson 06 · Chords

Four chords, and most of pop music

You started this course knowing C chords. Here is what that was the first quarter of.

A triad is a stack of thirds

Every chord you'll play for a long while is a triad: three notes, built by taking a note and stacking every other letter on top of it. Start on C, skip D, take E, skip F, take G. C–E–G. That's it — that's the C chord you already know, and now you know why it's those three notes (Triad, Wikipedia).

The three notes have names worth knowing, because the middle one does all the work:

Root, third, fifth Root — the note the chord is named after.
Third — the middle note. This is the one that decides major or minor.
Fifth — the top note. Same in both; it just holds the chord up.

Major is 4 semitones then 3. Minor is 3 then 4. Move that middle note down one key and a major chord becomes minor — that is the entire difference between happy and sad in Western music, and it's one key.

Build them from the symbol

A chord symbol is an instruction, not a name to memorise. C means "major triad on C". Am means "minor triad on A". Once you can turn the symbol into three notes, you can read a lead sheet — which is how most non-classical music is actually written down.

The four chords

Those four — C, G, Am, F — are not a random set. They are the I, V, vi and IV of C major: the first, fifth, sixth and fourth notes of the scale, each with a triad built on top. In that particular order (I–V–vi–IV) they are the most documented progression in popular music — Wikipedia lists over 300 songs built on it, and a comedy band got 100 million views stringing dozens of them into one medley to make the point.

A note on the order You wrote this down as I–IV–V–vi. Same four chords — the order is what changes between songs, and every rotation of these four is used somewhere. I–V–vi–IV is the one with the enormous song list attached. Learn the four shapes and you can play any rotation.

The change is the skill

Here's the thing nobody tells beginners: you will not be stuck because you can't find F. You'll be stuck because getting from C to F takes a second and a half, and the song doesn't wait.

So this drill measures change time — how long from finishing one chord to finishing the next. That number, not accuracy, is what decides whether you can play a song.

What the left hand does

Playing block chords in both hands sounds like a hymn. What makes a piano part sound like a record is the left hand breaking the chord up while the right hand holds the tune. Three patterns, in the order they should be learned:

PatternOrderSounds like
BlockAll three togetherHymn, ballad, Kanye
BrokenRoot, third, fifthGentle, folky
AlbertiLow, high, middle, highClassical — it's the engine under Mozart

Alberti bass is the odd one: lowest, highest, middle, highest, over and over (Alberti bass, Wikipedia). On a C chord that's C–G–E–G. It sounds far more elaborate than it is, which is exactly why it was so popular.

Check yourself

Between now and next time

Ten minutes: three on the progression drill until the change time drops under two seconds, three on Alberti with your left hand, and four just playing — C, G, Am, F round and round, left hand block chords, right hand doing whatever it likes on the white keys. It will sound like music, because it is.

Then try it on a song. Say You Will and See Me Again are both slow and sparse — put the recording on, find the chord changes by ear, and see how often they're one of these four shapes moved somewhere else.

Where this is going Next is key signatures and the circle of fifths — why some songs live on black keys, and how the same four shapes move to any key. That's what Arctic Nights needs: it lives in E minor, leaning on F♯ and D♯.
