Lesson 07 · Reading

Five anchors, two stories, one mirror

You told me exactly which notes stuck and why. That diagnosis is the whole lesson.

You found a real flaw, so let's fix it

Your words: you remember G because of the hook, C because it's the bottom, and Bass F because of the dots. Every note that came with a picture stuck. Every note that didn't, didn't.

That isn't a memory problem. It's a design problem in Lesson 2, and it's mine. I gave you three anchors and said everything else is a distance from one of them. But measure it: with only Bass F, Middle C and Treble G, the notes at the edges of your playing range sit three steps from the nearest anchor — and three steps is far enough that you stop stepping and start counting, which is the exact habit anchors exist to kill.

The two worst-served notes were C3 and C5. Which is convenient, because those are the two I'm about to add.

The fix Five anchors instead of three, so nothing in your playing range is more than two steps from one. And all five come from just two stories — not five things to memorise.

Story 1 · The three Cs are a ladder

C shows up three times on the grand staff, evenly spaced an octave apart, one per staff plus the one in the middle:

  • C5 — treble staff, third space.
  • C4, middle C — on its own short line between the staves.
  • C3 — bass staff, second space.

Middle C is the only note in this whole system with its own private line — that little stroke belongs to neither staff. That's your picture for it, and it's a better one than "it's the bottom", because it stays true when the note appears above the bass staff instead of below the treble.

Story 2 · Each clef points at its own note

You already have this one for the bass clef. It generalises:

ClefIts real nameWhat it's pointing at
TrebleThe G clefThe hook curls around the line that is G
BassThe F clefThe two dots straddle the line that is F

So the "hook" you already use isn't a coincidence you happened to notice — it's the clef's entire job. It's an arrow, drawn on every page of music, pointing at one note. Two anchors, permanently free.

The mirror — learn half, get half free

Here's the trick that does the most work, and it's the one nobody told you: the grand staff is symmetric around middle C.

Every anchor above middle C has a partner exactly the same number of staff positions below it:

AboveSteps outBelow
Treble G — the hook4 ↔ 4Bass F — the dots
C5 — treble 3rd space7 ↔ 7C3 — bass 2nd space
E4 — treble bottom line2 ↔ 2A3 — bass top line
F5 — treble top line10 ↔ 10G2 — bass bottom line

The two clefs are not two unrelated alphabets you have to learn twice. They are the same picture, reflected. Anything you can find above middle C, you can find the same distance below by looking at the mirrored spot — which is worth a great deal to you specifically, because your left hand reads about 37% slower than your right.

Shapes, not letters

The other half of "make it automatic" is to stop reading notes one at a time. Steps and skips each have a look, and the look is faster than the letter:

MoveWhat you seeWhat your hand does
StepLine → space, or space → line. A zigzag.Next white key, neighbouring finger.
SkipLine → line, or space → space. A stack.Miss a key, leave a finger gap.
The snowman A triad in root position — the chords you built in Lesson 6 — is three noteheads all on lines or all on spaces. Three circles stacked straight up: a snowman. Once you see snowmen, you stop reading chords note by note and start reading them as one object, which is the same jump you already made from letters to intervals.

Drill the five

Same drill as Lesson 2, wider net. Turn on Hints for the first run — it echoes each key you play onto the staff with its name, which is precisely the "welding" that makes a note automatic. Then turn it off, because reading is what happens when nothing echoes back.

When those are instant, add the notes one step either side — fourteen notes, and every one of them is reached by stepping off an anchor, never by counting from the bottom of the staff.

Check yourself

Between now and next time

Ten minutes, and this one is mostly away from the screen:

  1. Three minutes on the anchor drill, Hints on for the first run and off for the rest.
  2. Two minutes naming the stories out loud — point at each anchor on the staff map and say what it is and why. Saying it is what turns a picture into recall.
  3. Five minutes reading — Lesson 3, left hand, now using the mirror: when a bass note stalls you, find the matching spot above middle C and reflect it.
